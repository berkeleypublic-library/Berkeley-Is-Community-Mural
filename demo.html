A community mural at Berkeley Public Library, downtown branch

Berkeley Isxučyun

A collaborative public artwork by Berkeley Youth Works artists, landscape designer and muralist Hannah Pae, Café Ohlone, and the Library's Equity, Diversity & Inclusion team. Funded by the Berkeley Public Library Foundation, it is installed in the third-floor corridor between the downtown Central Library building and the Community Meeting Room.

Walk through the mural See who made it possible
Land acknowledgment

Berkeley Public Library buildings are built on the territory of xučyun (hooch-yoon), within the homeland of the Chochenyo (cho-chen-yo) speaking Ohlone (oh-low-nee) people. We acknowledge and honor the Ohlone people, who have lived in Berkeley and throughout the East Bay since time immemorial. The Library recognizes that Berkeley residents have and continue to benefit from the use and occupation of unceded stolen land, and we pledge to recognize the permanence and the rich contributions that the Ohlone people continue to bring to the City of Berkeley and throughout the East Bay.

A living document, co-written by Ohlone cultural leaders Vincent Medina and Louis Trevino together with the Berkeley Public Library.

What the mural represents

Honoring the past, designing the future

Together, we explored Berkeley's landscapes, histories, cultures, and communities to create a work that honors the past while imagining the future. Through research, interviews, archival materials, sketching, photography, and shared storytelling, the mural weaves together Indigenous knowledge, neighborhood histories, everyday moments, and the creativity of young people who call Berkeley home.

As visitors move through the corridor, they're invited to slow down, look closely, and consider the many stories that shape Berkeley, including their own. It celebrates the library as a place where community memory is preserved, new ideas take root, and everyone has a role in shaping the future of Berkeley.

The process

Field trips, archives, and a lot of sketching

Before a single wall was painted, the team walked Berkeley's streets, toured its murals, sat with Ohlone knowledge-keepers, and spent hours in the Library's own history room.

Walk the corridor

The mural, wall by wall

The mural unfolds across three connected walls as you move through the link corridor toward the Community Meeting Room.

Part One

The entrance

Manzanita trees with green leaves frame the doorway. An orange and gray coyote curls along the bottom of the wall, its head on the right and its body continuing to the left, forming an endless cycle. Above the door: "Berkeley Is," and its Chochenyo translation below.

Part Two

A map of Berkeley

The large central wall is arranged like a map, with community members, wildlife, and native plants throughout. Otters, grizzly bears, a salamander, quail, and red ants share the wall with red baskets, Ohlone dwellings, and tule boats. Landmarks include the Campanile, Berkeley High School, the Library, the Clock Tower, the hills, and a slice of the bay. A rising sun and a poem crown the whole scene.

Part Three

Continuing the story

A more collaged wall carries the manzanita trees forward, alongside a grizzly bear and California quail, a black-and-white basket, and butterflies circling a torch topped with another poem. Ohlone huts, Berkeley High, the Library, and tracings of community members tie this wall back to the rest of the corridor.

Who made it

Meet the artists

Hannah Pae

Muralist & landscape designer, Los Angeles

Hannah Pae is an LA-based artist and designer who has advocated for and contributed to many projects throughout California, including co-designing the outdoor space of Café Ohlone on the UC Berkeley campus. She's a landscape designer at Terremoto Landscape, a community land steward at the Burn Scar Test Plot in Los Angeles, co-founder of SSI YA GI, a community of Korean culinary culture, and a committee member of GYOPO, a Los Angeles-based nonprofit supporting diasporic Korean arts and culture.

Her passion for ecology and community runs through everything she makes: this mural, her landscape work, and her collaborations with other cultural stewards like Café Ohlone.

D'Aryn and Ariba asked not to be photographed for this page, so instead of portraits, their cards below lead with a piece of their own work from the mural.

D'Aryn Brooks Jr.

Youth artist, junior at Berkeley High School

D'Aryn came to a second mural project unsure his motivation would hold: some weeks he draws every day, others not at all. Field trips around Berkeley and time in the Library's history room changed that. He was struck by how much political history the archive held, much of it never digitized, and by how little-known Ohlone history is, "when they are the ones who built the foundation of this city."

Two pieces stayed with him most: a drawing of Berkeley High's courtyard, tied to his own middle school graduation, and a sketch made on the spot at the UC Berkeley clock tower, looking out over the bay.

My name is D'Aryn Brooks Jr. I'm a junior at Berkeley high school and I have a variety of hobbies and interests that have shaped my life, tigers and big cats especially, the Amur tiger most of all. I also listen to a wide range of music, from rap and soul to jazz, rock, and metal. My interest in art has taken up a great part of my life, even if it isn't always consistent.

Growing up, my motivation for art has come and gone in waves. So being assigned another mural project after last summer's felt intimidating at first. I expected it to be boring, but the planning activities sparked my inspiration to draw again.

One trip took us around Telegraph Avenue to look at other murals. My group noticed the smallest details, colors, expressions, scale, even the obstacles like power boxes, and how those details carried meaning. We used that idea instead of avoiding it: rather than dodging obstacles on our own wall, we built them in.

On a later trip to the UC Berkeley campus, a place I'd been to plenty of times, I was still struck by the history behind the plant life and biodiversity, a sense of collectiveness in the city I'd overlooked. It made me want to pay closer attention to Berkeley, whether I'm in a park or just walking around noticing what's happening.

Inside the library, my biggest interest became the political history and community of Berkeley, much of it hidden from the internet, including political groups that went by different names so they wouldn't get caught. Learning about Ohlone culture through the archive's films and books was just as striking. It's a shame how little people know about a culture that built the foundation of this city and the whole bay area.

Two pieces from the weeks of drawing mean the most to me. The first is a drawing of Berkeley High's courtyard and auditorium, a place with real history in the city, and personal history for me, since it hosted my middle school graduation. Getting the scale and depth of the buildings right took a lot of trial and error, and it still isn't perfect, but I still like it. The second is a sketch from a field trip to UC Berkeley's clock tower, looking down the street that divides campus with a view of the bay behind it. I was surprised how motivated I got just from that view alone.

Drawing for this mural has been enjoyable. I'm grateful I got to take part in it, learn more about what to do with my skills, and see the work go up somewhere the public will actually see it. I never would have believed my art would end up on this mural.

Ariba Talukder

Youth artist, senior at Berkeley High School

Ariba joined as an EDI Intern through the YouthWorks program. The team researched Berkeley's history in the Library's history room, sketched important spots around the city, took a mural tour for inspiration, and interviewed community members about life in Berkeley. A field trip to the Lawrence Hall of Science brought the group face to face with Vincent Medina and Louis Trevino of Café Ohlone, who taught a few words of Chochenyo and shared Ohlone plant knowledge and history.

She spent the most time on the Sather Gate drawing, mapped out carefully for accurate, symmetrical proportions, which she sees as representing both UC Berkeley's history and the protests that have long taken place in front of it, on land that remains the ancestral homeland of the Ohlone people.

I am a senior at Berkeley High School. I became an EDI Intern through the YouthWorks program and was selected as one of the interns to help create this mural. Throughout the project, we researched Berkeley's history in the library's history room, visited important spots around the city to sketch them, took a mural tour for inspiration, and interviewed community members about their perspectives on living in Berkeley.

This internship felt different from any other at the Berkeley Public Library. I didn't expect to be so involved in creating the mural, let alone get to draw some of its key pieces, and the research work before the drawing began was just as engaging.

What I liked most were the field trips: walking around Berkeley, stopping to sketch the places we wanted to include. On one trip, we went to the Lawrence Hall of Science and met Vincent Medina and Louis Trevino, and learned about Ohlone culture from an indigenous perspective, a few words of Chochenyo, native plants, and a deeper understanding of Ohlone history.

The experience was educational both for the history and for what I learned about my own art. We drew for a week straight, and consistent practice actually improved my skills. I got to understand my own artistic process and figure out which techniques work for me. I don't plan to pursue art as a career, but I want to keep it as something I enjoy and keep sharing, even as a hobby rather than a profession. This is the first big art project I've been part of, and I'm grateful for the opportunity.

As an Asian American, spending time in the history room was meaningful, learning about the contributions Asian communities have made to Berkeley, even where historical records don't always represent every group. The room still holds documents and archival photos that recognize the struggles and contributions of many communities, and I think it represents Berkeley's history and culture well.

I spent the most time on the Sather Gate drawing. Unlike some of my other pieces, I wanted this one accurate and symmetrical, so I mapped it carefully, simplifying some smaller details, which is part of my usual style. I'd sketched Sather Gate before, in my own notebook, during our field trips with Hannah. To me it's the most recognizable part of UC Berkeley and represents the university as a whole. It stands on the ancestral homeland of the Ohlone people, with Strawberry Creek running through campus nearby, and it's long been a place where Berkeley's people gather to protest and speak up. I gave the color palette less attention here; the structure's actual color is already so recognizable that it didn't need much interpretation.

There's a lot to learn here, and I'm grateful to have interned at the Library again, this time as part of something even bigger.

In partnership with

Knowledge-keepers and collaborators

Café Ohlone

Created by Vincent Medina and Louis Trevino, Café Ohlone is a cultural institution sharing the culinary practices and culture of the Ohlone people, how food is made, where it comes from, and where it's eaten. In 2017 the pair founded mak-'amham ("our food"), leading cooking classes, gathering trips, community dinners, weekly language classes, and cultural sessions. In 2018 they opened Café Ohlone at UC Berkeley, next to an academic department that had once declared the Ohlone people extinct, a seasonal restaurant decorated with native plants, napa basalt boulder seating, East Bay redwood tables, and abalone finery. They also manage Ammatka Café at the Lawrence Hall of Science.

Medina and Trevino generously advised this mural, supporting the Library's ongoing acknowledgment of harm and its work toward reconciliation between the City of Berkeley and the Ohlone people.

Equity, Diversity & Inclusion at Berkeley Public Library

The EDI Division works across the organization so that equity isn't a separate initiative, but a core responsibility embedded in how the Library hires and supports staff, develops policy, designs services, builds collections, allocates resources, engages the community, and shapes its public spaces, guided by the Library's Board-adopted harm statement, equity commitments, strategic plan, and the Government Alliance on Race and Equity (GARE) framework.

It's the same understanding of the Library as public infrastructure, a civic institution where people can see themselves represented, build skills and relationships, and help shape the shared life of Berkeley, is what this mural was built to express.
